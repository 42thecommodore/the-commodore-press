Commodore Press
A working library · Luca

Read the sources. Mark what is still argued.

Five wings of one method. Every claim here carries where it came from and where specialists disagree — because the disagreement is usually the part worth having.

Colophon

This is a library, not a credential. It holds what I have read, what I think it means, and — deliberately, in every wing — the places where the people who know most still disagree. A claim without its dispute is half a claim.

The five wings are one method applied to five kinds of material. The Press is ideas; Lives is people worth a whole book; The Atlas is the sources I actually listen to and the principles more than one of them arrived at independently; The Field Manuals are what I hold, written as positions rather than advice; The Slipway is the trade book — the operating lessons of people who build things, kept the way sailors kept a chandlery: everything you need before setting out, each item marked with where it came from.

Set in EB Garamond and IBM Plex Mono. Assembled by hand and with AI assistance; figures are checked against a named source, and where I have corrected myself the correction stays visible rather than being quietly patched. The reading wings are set on paper. The Atlas is the only room kept dark, because a star chart is the one thing that genuinely needs a night sky.

The portraits in Lives are public-domain engravings, paintings and photographs, reduced to the house duotone and set as plates. A life without a safely public image carries the press mark instead of a borrowed picture, which seems the more honest trade. One life is in that position by decision: Henrietta Lacks, whose entry says why at length, and whose estate has itself disputed the use of family photographs. Eight more carry the mark for duller reasons, and they are not the same reason. Florence Nightingale, Ada Lovelace and Ida B. Wells all have portraits old enough to be public domain, and the plates have not been cut yet. Barbara McClintock and Rose Blumkin died recently enough that most photographs of them are still in copyright, which is a licence problem rather than a scheduling one. Tu Youyou and Dolores Huerta are both alive, and a free image of a living person is harder to establish than a dead one's. No likeness of Ibn al-Haytham survives at all — every picture in circulation, the one on the Iraqi banknote included, was imagined long afterwards — so that mark is likely permanent. A gap the house is working on is printed as a gap, not quietly filled. Two plates are licensed rather than public domain, and are credited here because the licence requires it: the Mandela photograph is by South Africa The Good News (sagoodnews.co.za) under CC BY 2.0, and the Steve Jobs photograph was taken at WWDC in 2010 by Matthew Yohe under CC BY-SA 3.0. One more is neither: the 1939 photograph of Robert Moses is by C. M. Stieglitz for the New York World-Telegram & Sun, held by the Library of Congress, which states that it knows of no copyright restrictions on it — a narrower claim than public domain, and worth saying in those words. Everything else came through Wikimedia Commons.

Wing III · the sources · kept dark

Debts & Lessons

A constellation is a principle more than one source handed me independently, across unrelated lives — a lion tracker, a surgeon, a fintech founder arriving at the same line. The repetitions are the part I trust. These are sources I listened to, not verdicts on the people — and the wing takes its name from Marcus Aurelius, whose Meditations open with a book of exactly this: debts and lessons, with names attached.

Wing IV · the doctrine

The Field Manuals

Four volumes of what I hold — written as positions I can be argued out of, not advice for anyone else. Where a position comes from someone, they are named; where I think it is over-claimed, that is marked too.

Wing V · the trade book

The Slipway

Where ships get built — as a set of ideas, not a shipyard tour. This wing is a commonplace book of the craft: operating lessons collected from people who have actually crossed the water, kept because each one changed what I did next. The other four wings are the chart room; this one is the toolbox.
